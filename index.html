01

How a BCG-style case works

In a BCG-style case, you drive. After the prompt, you lay out a structure, decide where to dig first, and ask for the data you need. The interviewer hands you charts and pushes on your logic.

Put simply, they want to see you lead a messy problem to a clear answer, read charts quickly, and use good business judgment. Formats vary by office, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Many offices use an online test first, like Casey, a chatbot case of about 30 minutes.
  • Live interviews run about 45 minutes: around 30 for the case, and 10 to 15 for fit questions.
  • Usually two or three interviews in the first round, then two or three in the final round.

What they look for

You lead, they test

  • Leading the case: you choose the next step, and say why.
  • Reading exhibits fast: charts and tables come often.
  • Math built into the questions, done cleanly.
  • Business judgment: does the answer make sense for this client?

What the case feels like

  • Structure. You set the plan and say where you'd start, with a reason.
  • Ask for data. "Can I see attendance over the last few years?" Specific asks get useful charts.
  • Read the chart. Lead with the one or two things that matter.
  • Do the math. Round numbers, every step out loud.
  • Recommend. The answer first, then reasons, risks, and next steps.

The shape of the case

Ten terms to drop naturally

Profit tree

Profit equals revenue minus costs. Then split each side again: revenue into price times volume, costs into fixed and variable.

Price × volume

Revenue is what you charge times how many you sell. Always check both before deciding which one broke.

Contribution per customer

What each extra customer adds to profit after variable costs. At Starlight, about 9 dollars per visitor.

Operating leverage

When most costs are fixed, a small drop in customers causes a big drop in profit. It works the other way too.

Utilization

How much of your capacity gets used, like the share of seats filled at a theater.

Revenue sharing

Splitting sales with a partner. Theaters pay film studios a big share of every ticket.

Premiumization

Getting customers to trade up to a better, pricier product, like recliner seats and giant screens.

Cannibalization

When a new product takes sales from your own existing product instead of winning new customers.

Dynamic pricing

Changing prices with demand: cheaper on a quiet Tuesday, full price on a Friday night.

Membership

A monthly fee for a set number of visits. It brings steady revenue and gets people to come more often.

Restate the question in one sentence, then say what you'd like to clarify.

Strong answer

"So profit fell from 60 million to 20 million in three years, even with higher prices, and the CEO wants to know why, and how to get back to 60 million within three years. Is that right? Before I structure it, I have a few quick questions."

Interviewer: "Sure, go ahead." In a candidate-led case, the interviewer hands you the wheel right away. Taking it confidently is part of the test.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • Is the drop happening across all theaters, or in some?
    Across most of them, but it's worst in small-town locations.
  • Are competitors seeing the same thing?
    Yes, the whole industry has fewer visitors than a few years ago, but the top chains are recovering faster.
  • How does Starlight make money?
    Tickets and concessions. Studios take about 55 percent of ticket revenue. On snacks, Starlight keeps about 75 cents of every dollar after food costs.
  • What's on the table: closing theaters, investing?
    Everything. The board would fund up to 200 million dollars of investment.
  • Have costs changed?
    Wages and rent went up a little. Most costs, like rent and staff, are fixed.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like which movies did best last year. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Okay. How do you want to approach this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch your structure. Then walk through it, say where you'd start, and ask for specific data.

Strong framework

Why did Starlight's profit fall from $60M to $20M, and how does it get back?
1Revenue
  • Number of visitors
  • Ticket price per visitor
  • Snack spend per visitor
2Costs
  • Studios' share of tickets
  • Food costs (variable)
  • Rent, staff (fixed)
3Market
  • Industry attendance trend
  • Streaming and release timing
  • Rivals' moves, like premium screens
4Ways to fix it
  • Fill empty seats
  • Trade customers up
  • Cut what loses money

Say it like this

"Profit is revenue minus costs, so I'll split it that way. On revenue: the number of visitors, ticket price, and snack spend per visitor. On costs: the studios' share of tickets, food costs, and fixed costs like rent and staff. I'd also check the market, to see whether this is a Starlight problem or an industry problem. Then I'd look at ways to fix it. Since prices went up and profit still fell, my hypothesis is that visitors dropped while fixed costs stayed put. Could I see attendance and pricing over the last few years?"

Why it works

  • It's MECE: revenue and costs together cover all of profit.
  • It separates a Starlight problem from an industry problem.
  • It ends with a hypothesis and a specific data request. In a candidate-led case, that's you steering.

If you blank, the profit tree, revenue as price times volume and costs as fixed plus variable, works for any profitability case.

05

Exhibit 1: four years of trends

Interviewer

Good. Here's what you asked for, plus a few other measures. What stands out?

Which finding should you lead with?

Visitors dropped from 40 million to 30 million, down 25 percent. Profit dropped two-thirds, from 60 million to 20 million. That gap is operating leverage: rent and staff don't shrink when fewer people come. Ticket prices and snack spend both rose, which helped, but not enough. Two clues for later: only 16 percent of seats are filled, and premium screens keep growing their share, so customers will pay more for a better experience.

Then

Say the insight, tie it to the goal, and ask for your next piece of data.

Say it like this

"The big story is volume. Visitors are down 25 percent, but profit is down two-thirds, because most costs are fixed. Higher prices and snack spend helped but didn't close the gap. Two things give me hope: 84 percent of seats are empty, so there's room to fill them, and premium screens keep growing, so people will pay for a better experience. Can I see how much each factor moved profit?"

Notice the pattern: insight, so-what for the goal, then your own next ask. In a candidate-led case, that ask is how you move to Exhibit 2.

06

Exhibit 2: where the profit went

Interviewer

Here's the finance team's walk from 60 million to 20 million. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Higher ticket prices and snack spend added 60 million together. Fewer visitors took away 90 million. Each lost visitor costs about 9 dollars of profit: about 4 dollars and 50 cents from the ticket after the studios' share, and about 4 dollars and 50 cents from snacks. Raising prices again risks driving even more people away.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences, and propose what to test next.

Say it like this

"Volume is the problem. Losing 10 million visitors cost about 90 million of profit, while higher prices and snack spend only added about 60 million. With 84 percent of seats empty and fixed costs that don't move, filling seats is the best lever, and the premium trend suggests people will pay more for a better experience. I'd like to test whether converting screens to premium pays off."

Premium screens: do the math

Same skills as market sizing: state the formula, use round numbers, then sanity-check. The twist here is cannibalization.

Your move

Say the formula first: extra profit per screen equals premium profit minus regular profit. Then ask yourself what you might be double counting.

  1. 1 · Regular screen profit25,000 × $9$225K
  2. 2 · Premium screen profit30,000 × $15 = $450K, so +$225K a screen+$225K
  3. 3 · On paper$900K ÷ $225K = 4-year payback. $40M ÷ $225K ≈ 180 screens180
  4. 4 · Cannibalization10,000 who'd have come anyway × $9−$90K
  5. 5 · Real gain per screen$225K − $90K. Payback ≈ 7 years$135K
  6. 6 · Screens to close the gap$40M ÷ $135K ≈ 300 screens ≈ $270M. Over the $200M budget≈300

Quick check: how much extra profit does one conversion really add, after cannibalization?

The premium screen adds 225 thousand on paper. But 10 thousand of its visitors would have come anyway, bringing 9 dollars each, or 90 thousand. 225 minus 90 is 135 thousand. Everyday version: if a new menu item sells 30 a day, but 10 of those buyers would have ordered something else anyway, only 20 are truly new sales.

On paper = +$225K a screen Cannibalization = −$90K Real gain ≈ $135K, about 300 screens to close the gap
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"One conversion adds about 225 thousand dollars on paper, but about a third of premium visitors would have come anyway, so the real gain is about 135 thousand, a payback of about 7 years. Closing the 40 million gap with premium alone would take about 300 screens and 270 million, more than the 200 million budget. So premium is part of the answer, in the busiest theaters, not the whole answer."

If the interviewer asks why the cannibalization rate matters so much: "It's the difference between needing 180 screens and 300. I'd test it in a few theaters before committing the full budget."

08

Brainstorm, then prioritize

Interviewer

So premium screens won't close the gap on their own. What else could Starlight do? Brainstorm first. Then score the team's six ideas for ease and impact, from 1 to 5, and pick your top three.

Your move

Brainstorm in groups for 60 seconds. Use your profit tree: more visitors, more money per visitor, lower costs.

Strong brainstorm, grouped by the profit tree

More visitors

A monthly membership. Cheaper weekday tickets. Family deals. Showing live sports and concerts.

More money per visitor

Premium screens in busy theaters. Order food from your seat. A better menu, or a bar for adults.

Lower costs

Close or sublease theaters that lose money. Schedule staff to match busy hours. Cut energy use.

New revenue

More on-screen advertising. Private rentals for parties and companies. Gaming tournaments in the morning.

Using your profit tree as the groups shows the interviewer your ideas come from a structure, not a guess.

Then

Give one reason for each score, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with three. A monthly membership, because it fills empty seats and members still buy snacks. Cheaper weekday tickets, because they're easy and every extra visitor still adds about 9 dollars. And closing or subleasing the theaters that lose money, because it cuts fixed costs fast. Premium screens come next, but only in the busiest theaters."

    Which three come first?

    A, B, and C go straight at the problem: empty seats and fixed costs. D has the biggest impact per screen but is slow, costly, and cannibalizes, so do it selectively. E is easy but small. F is easy, but price increases already pushed visitors away.

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO just walked in. You have 60 seconds. What should Starlight do?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Starlight can get back to about 60 million in three years, but not by raising prices again. It needs to fill empty seats, cut what loses money, and go premium only where it pays."

    "First, fill seats. 84 percent of seats are empty, and each extra visitor adds about 9 dollars of profit. A monthly membership and cheaper weekday tickets could add about 15 million."

    "Second, cut what loses money. Closing or subleasing the 15 worst theaters removes their losses and fixed costs, worth about 10 million."

    "Third, go premium where it pays. Converting about 100 screens in the busiest theaters adds about 135 thousand each after cannibalization. That's about 15 million, for about 90 million of the 200 million budget."

    "The main risk is that the membership mostly attracts people who would have paid full price anyway. So I'd test membership and weekday pricing in 10 theaters first. Next steps: launch that test, start lease talks on the 15 worst theaters, and pick the first 30 screens to convert."

    The three-year plan

    Year 1Fill seats
    • Test membership and weekday pricing in 10 theaters, then roll out.
    • Start lease talks on the 15 worst theaters.
    • Convert the first 30 premium screens.
    Year 2Cut and convert
    • Close or sublease the 15 theaters.
    • Convert 40 more premium screens.
    • Add seat-side food ordering.
    Year 3Scale
    • Convert the last 30 premium screens.
    • Adjust membership pricing.
    • Review the next weakest theaters.

    Indicators to track

    • Share of seats filled. Target: from 16 percent up to 19 percent.
    • Members, and how often they come each month.
    • Extra profit per premium screen. Target: at least 135 thousand dollars.
    • Theaters losing money. Target: zero.

    Bonus drills: market sizing and growth

    Two short reps in case the interviewer switches to a classic consulting question.

    Market sizing: U.S. movie tickets

    Interviewer

    Estimate how much Americans spend on movie tickets in a year.

    Your move

    Go top-down. Start with the population, narrow to moviegoers, then multiply by trips and price. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Start broadU.S. population≈330M
    2. × 60% go at least once a yearMoviegoers≈200M
    3. × 4 trips a yearTickets sold≈800M
    4. × $11 a ticket800M × $11≈$8.8B
    5. Sanity checkRecent U.S. box office: high single-digit billions✓
    6. Starlight check30M ÷ 800M ≈ 4%. Fits a mid-size chain✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Market growth: will moviegoing come back?

    Interviewer

    Do you think theater attendance will recover? What does that mean for Starlight?

    Your move

    Split growth into more visitors and more spend per visitor, then list drivers and headwinds.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more visitors × more spend per visitor
    Event films

    Big releases still pull crowds that want to see them first, on a big screen.

    Premium formats

    Giant screens and recliners offer something people can't get at home.

    Better food and seats

    Higher spend per visitor, even if visits don't fully recover.

    New events

    Concerts, live sports, and anime nights fill screens between big films.

    Headwinds

    • Films arrive on streaming sooner, so many people wait.
    • Fewer films are released than before.
    • Younger people spend more time on games and social video.

    Quick math

    If visitors keep falling 8 percent a year, how long until they halve? Use the rule of 72: 72 divided by 8 is 9 years.

    "I don't expect visits to return to their old level, but spend per visitor can keep rising. So Starlight should plan for a smaller, more premium business: fill the seats it has, close what doesn't pay, and charge more where the experience is better."
